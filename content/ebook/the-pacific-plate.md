# The Pacific Plate

*30 Polynesian recipes for the modern kitchen*

---

## What this book is, and what it isn't

This is not a wellness book. It is not a longevity manifesto. It is not
a guide to eating your way out of any specific medical condition. It is
a cookbook.

What it is, specifically, is a working translation of the ancestral
Polynesian diet into recipes you can cook in a normal kitchen with
ingredients you can mostly find at a normal grocery store. Where an
ingredient is hard to source, we name the substitution and the trade-off
it makes. Where a recipe is built on something genuinely worth the
extra effort — fresh fish from a real fish counter, a specific salt, a
particular kind of rice — we say so and explain why.

We are not Polynesian. Pacific Nutra is a publishing project, not a
cultural institution. We have been cooking from the Pacific for years,
have read the ethnographic and nutritional research carefully, and
take pains to credit the people, regions, and traditions these recipes
come from. Where we get something wrong, we want to hear about it —
the email at the back of the book reaches a human who reads everything.

## Why this diet, factually

Pre-contact Polynesian populations had some of the lowest rates of
cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and metabolic disorder ever recorded
in human history. We know this because Western traders, missionaries,
and physicians documented what they ate in unusual detail between the
1700s and the early 1900s, and because medical records exist from both
sides of the dietary transition when Western processed foods arrived.

Within two generations of the introduction of refined flour, refined
sugar, and canned and packaged foods, Pacific Islander populations
moved from some of the *healthiest* on the planet to some of the most
metabolically burdened. The reversal is one of the cleanest
demonstrations in nutritional epidemiology that what you eat matters,
and that it matters at the population level, not just the individual.

The food that did the work isn't exotic. It's five categories of food
you've probably eaten in some form already.

## The five pillars

1. **Starchy roots and tubers** — taro (kalo), sweet potato (ʻuala),
   yam (uhi), cassava. Cooked, pounded, fermented, or fried. Slow-
   burning carbohydrates with significant mineral content and a low
   glycemic load.

2. **Breadfruit (ulu)** — calorically generous, technically a fruit,
   functionally a starch. Bakes like a potato, fries like a yuca, and
   in its ripe form, sweetens into something close to custard.

3. **Fish and seafood** — fresh, raw, smoked, salted. Reef fish, tuna,
   octopus, shellfish. The omega-3 intake of a traditional Pacific
   Islander dwarfs that of a typical modern American or European.

4. **Leafy greens** — taro leaves, watercress, pohole fern (Hawaii),
   long-leaf chicory and others. Often cooked with coconut milk or
   wrapped around fish for steaming.

5. **Coconut** — flesh, milk, water, oil. Present in some form at
   nearly every meal. The medium-chain triglycerides in coconut fat
   behave very differently from seed oils, and modern nutrition has
   walked back four decades of unwarranted villainization.

## What you'll notice is missing

- **Wheat flour.** No bread, no pasta, no pastry in the traditional
  diet. Carbohydrates came from whole roots.
- **Refined sugar.** Sweetness came from fruit, coconut, and the
  occasional honey raid.
- **Dairy.** Polynesia is one of the few traditional cultures with no
  history of dairy consumption.
- **Seed oils.** Cooking fat was coconut, pork fat, and fish oil.

You will notice we have not removed these things from this book either,
because this is a cookbook for the world you live in, not the one you
might. Most recipes include a "modern variation" that uses what's in
your kitchen. Where the substitution genuinely changes the dish in a
way you should know about, we mention it.

## How to use this book

The thirty recipes are organized in six sections:

1. **Roots & tubers** — five recipes. Start here. The Pacific
   pantry is built on starchy roots, and once you've roasted a
   breadfruit, the rest of the book gets easier.
2. **From the sea** — six recipes. Poke, lomi salmon, coconut-
   ginger shrimp, whole grilled fish.
3. **Greens** — five recipes. Coconut-creamed kalo leaves,
   watercress with sesame, ginger-garlic bok choy.
4. **Coconut** — five recipes. Coconut rice, haupia, toasted
   coconut chips, coconut-poached fish.
5. **One-pot meals** — five recipes that combine the pillars.
   Kalua-style pork, coconut curry with taro, four kinds of poke
   bowl.
6. **Sweet** — four recipes. Haupia, banana lumpia, shave ice,
   mochi.

Each recipe includes:

- A short note on where it comes from
- A list of ingredients with sourcing notes
- Method, written for someone who can cook but isn't a chef
- A "modern variation" for the most common ingredient substitutions
- A serving suggestion — what to plate it with from elsewhere in
  the book

Total cooking time across the book sits between 20 minutes (a poke
bowl, a haupia) and three hours (a kalua pork). Most recipes are in
the 30–45 minute range.

## A short note on sourcing

We've written a short sourcing guide at the back of the book. The
short version:

- **Taro and breadfruit** — large Asian and Pacific grocers in any
  US coastal city carry frozen taro and breadfruit. The Hawaiian Ulu
  Cooperative ships fresh breadfruit nationwide.
- **Fresh fish** — find a real fish counter, not a frozen-fish
  section. Whole Foods will do; a local Asian seafood market will be
  better.
- **Coconut** — full-fat canned coconut milk (we like Aroy-D and
  Native Forest) is fine. Don't use the watery "lite" stuff.
- **Hawaiian sea salt** — ʻAlaea red salt and the white Hawaiian
  sea salt from Maui are both worth keeping. We use them as
  finishing salts; for cooking, kosher salt is fine.
- **Coconut aminos** — the soy-free umami of choice. Coconut Secret
  is the standard.

If you can't find something, the modern variation will get you
within shouting distance of the original. The recipes work.

## 1.1 · Roasted breadfruit wedges with ʻAlaea salt

> A breadfruit roasted in its skin is, structurally, the best
> potato you've ever had. The interior is fluffy, slightly sweet,
> and holds an aggressive seasoning the way few starches can.

**Serves 4 as a side, 2 as a main**
**Time:** 50 minutes (10 active)

### Ingredients

- 1 medium breadfruit (about 2–3 lbs), green to slightly yellow
- 3 tbsp coconut oil, melted
- 1 tsp ʻAlaea Hawaiian sea salt (or flaky sea salt)
- 1 tsp freshly cracked black pepper
- Optional: 1 lime, cut into wedges for serving

### Method

1. Heat the oven to 400°F (200°C).
2. Cut the breadfruit in half lengthwise, then into eighths. You're
   going for wedges roughly the size of a thick steak fry. Leave
   the skin on.
3. Toss the wedges in melted coconut oil, salt, and pepper. Spread
   on a baking sheet in a single layer, cut sides down.
4. Roast 35–40 minutes, flipping halfway. The wedges are done when
   the cut sides are deep golden and the flesh yields easily to a
   fork.
5. Sprinkle with another small pinch of ʻAlaea salt while still
   hot. Serve with lime wedges if you like a little acid.

### Modern variation

No breadfruit? Yuca (cassava) is the closest analog. Peel and cut
the same way, but parboil 5 minutes before roasting — yuca's
denser and won't soften enough at this temperature otherwise.

Sweet potato will also work and is more widely available, but the
texture and flavor are different enough that you'll have made a
different dish. Still good.

### What to plate it with

The roasted-breadfruit wedge is a side dish. Pair with **1.3
sweet-potato and ginger soup** for an all-vegetable meal, or with
**2.4 coconut-ginger shrimp** for something heartier.

## 1.2 · Taro mash with brown butter and chives

> The Hawaiian word for taro is *kalo*. The cooked, mashed form is
> called *paʻiʻai* when thick, *poi* when thinned with water and
> fermented. This recipe sits in between — closer to mashed potato
> in spirit, but with the slightly nutty, mineral flavor of taro
> and the gentle gloss of brown butter on top.

**Serves 4**
**Time:** 45 minutes

### Ingredients

- 1.5 lbs taro root (about 2 medium roots), peeled and cut into
  1-inch chunks
- 1 tsp kosher salt, plus more to taste
- 4 tbsp unsalted butter
- ¼ cup full-fat coconut milk, warmed
- 2 tbsp finely sliced chives
- Freshly ground black pepper

**Note on raw taro:** Raw taro contains calcium oxalate and is
irritating to skin and mucous membranes. Wear gloves while peeling
if you have sensitive skin, and never taste it raw. Cooking
destroys the irritant completely.

### Method

1. Place the taro chunks in a pot, cover with cold water by an
   inch, add the salt. Bring to a boil, reduce to a steady simmer,
   and cook 20–25 minutes until a knife slides through with no
   resistance.
2. While the taro cooks, brown the butter: melt it in a small
   light-colored pan over medium heat. After 4–6 minutes the milk
   solids will turn golden brown and the kitchen will smell like
   toffee. Pull immediately off the heat — it goes from brown to
   black quickly. Scrape into a small bowl, brown bits and all.
3. Drain the taro thoroughly. Return to the warm pot and mash with
   a potato masher or wooden spoon. Taro is starchier than potato,
   so it mashes a little smoother and stickier.
4. Stir in the warmed coconut milk, half the brown butter, salt to
   taste, and a few cranks of pepper.
5. Plate, spoon the remaining brown butter over the top, scatter
   the chives.

### Modern variation

If you can't find fresh taro, frozen taro chunks (available at
Asian groceries) work — boil from frozen, add 5 minutes to the
cook time. Avoid taro powder for this recipe; it's too fine and
sets up like a paste.

### What to plate it with

A perfect companion to **2.5 whole grilled fish with banana leaf**
or **5.1 kalua-style pork**. Also good with **3.4 ginger-garlic
bok choy** for an all-vegetable plate.

## 1.3 · Sweet potato and ginger soup

> The sweet potato (`ʻuala` in Hawaiian, `kumara` in Maori) is the
> only one of the Pacific staple roots that's now widely available
> in any supermarket. This soup uses two pounds of it, plus a
> generous knob of fresh ginger, plus coconut milk, and is on the
> table in 30 minutes.

**Serves 4–6**
**Time:** 30 minutes

### Ingredients

- 2 lbs Japanese (purple-skinned, yellow-flesh) or orange sweet
  potatoes, peeled and cut into 1-inch chunks
- 1 medium yellow onion, roughly chopped
- 4 cloves garlic, smashed
- 1 thumb-sized (~2-inch) piece fresh ginger, peeled and sliced
- 1 can (14 oz) full-fat coconut milk
- 4 cups (1 quart) chicken or vegetable stock
- 2 tbsp coconut oil
- 1 tsp kosher salt
- ½ tsp freshly ground black pepper
- Optional: 1 tsp red pepper flakes, lime wedges, toasted coconut
  flakes for garnish

### Method

1. In a heavy pot or Dutch oven, warm the coconut oil over medium
   heat. Add the onion and a pinch of salt; cook 4–5 minutes
   until softened.
2. Add the garlic and ginger; cook 1 minute more, stirring, until
   fragrant.
3. Add the sweet potato chunks and the stock. Bring to a boil,
   reduce to a steady simmer, cover, and cook 15–18 minutes until
   the sweet potato is completely tender.
4. Off the heat, stir in the coconut milk, salt, pepper, and red
   pepper flakes if using.
5. Blend smooth with an immersion blender (or, working in batches,
   a regular blender — leave the lid vented and start slow).
6. Taste, adjust salt. Serve with a lime wedge and a sprinkle of
   toasted coconut flakes.

### Modern variation

Stand-in for the coconut milk: heavy cream works but changes the
character significantly. The soup becomes richer and more western.
Not bad — just different.

For a heartier version, top each bowl with **2.4 coconut-ginger
shrimp** or shredded leftover chicken.

### What to plate it with

Crusty bread is the standard western pairing but isn't quite
right here. A side of **1.1 roasted breadfruit wedges** is more
in keeping. Or simply rice.

## 1.4 · Cassava fries with garlic and lime

> Cassava (yuca) is one of the oldest cultivated foods in the
> world, grown across the tropics including the Pacific. As a
> fry, it's denser and chewier than potato — somewhere between
> a thick steak fry and a polenta chip. Forgiving to cook,
> aggressively friendly to bold seasoning.

**Serves 4**
**Time:** 35 minutes

### Ingredients

- 1.5 lbs cassava (yuca) root — fresh or frozen, peeled, cut into
  thick fry shapes (about 1/2 × 1/2 × 3 inches)
- 1 tbsp kosher salt (for the boil)
- 4 cloves garlic, very finely minced
- Zest of 1 lime
- 2 tbsp finely chopped cilantro or parsley
- ½ tsp ʻAlaea sea salt (or flaky sea salt)
- ¼ tsp freshly cracked black pepper
- Coconut oil or neutral oil for frying (about 1 quart for deep-
  fry, or 4 tbsp for pan-fry)

### Method

1. Place the cassava in a pot, cover with cold water, add the
   tablespoon of kosher salt. Bring to a boil and cook 8–10
   minutes until just fork-tender but not falling apart. Drain,
   spread on a tray, let dry 10 minutes. (This parboil is non-
   negotiable — cassava needs it for both texture and food-safety
   reasons.)
2. **Deep-fry method:** heat the oil to 375°F. Fry the cassava
   in batches 4–5 minutes until deep golden. Drain on a wire
   rack.
3. **Pan-fry method:** heat 4 tbsp coconut oil in a large skillet
   (cast iron is ideal) over medium-high. Fry the cassava in a
   single layer, 3–4 minutes per side, until crisp on the
   outside.
4. While still hot, toss in a large bowl with the minced garlic,
   lime zest, cilantro, ʻAlaea salt, and pepper. Serve
   immediately.

### Modern variation

If you can only find frozen yuca, use that — it's already peeled
and cut, and works equally well. Skip the parboil step if the
package says "ready to cook."

For a smokier version, finish with a generous pinch of smoked
paprika and serve with **2.1 ahi poke** as a side.

### What to plate it with

Pair with any of the fish dishes from Section 2. Especially good
with **2.3 lomi salmon** — the cold, bright, acid-heavy salmon
plays well against the rich crisp fry.

## 1.5 · ʻUlu (breadfruit) "rice" with coconut and scallion

> A way to use ripe-but-not-yet-soft breadfruit that lands closer
> to fried rice than to a roasted side. The texture is rough,
> fluffy, and absorbs sauce beautifully.

**Serves 4**
**Time:** 35 minutes

### Ingredients

- 1 medium breadfruit (about 2 lbs), peeled, halved, seed core
  removed
- 3 tbsp coconut oil
- 4 scallions, sliced thin (white and green parts separated)
- 3 cloves garlic, minced
- 1 tbsp grated fresh ginger
- ¼ cup full-fat coconut milk
- 1 tbsp coconut aminos
- 1 tsp kosher salt
- Optional: 2 tbsp toasted unsweetened coconut flakes

### Method

1. Grate the peeled breadfruit on the large holes of a box grater,
   or pulse in a food processor until it resembles coarse rice. Do
   not overprocess into a paste.
2. Heat the coconut oil in a large skillet over medium. Add the
   white parts of the scallions, garlic, and ginger; cook 1
   minute until fragrant.
3. Add the grated breadfruit, the salt, and ½ cup of water. Stir
   to combine, cover, reduce heat to medium-low, and cook 10
   minutes, stirring once or twice. The breadfruit will steam-cook
   in its own moisture and the added water.
4. Uncover, raise the heat to medium-high. Stir in the coconut
   milk and coconut aminos. Cook another 3–4 minutes, stirring
   often, until any excess liquid has cooked off and the
   "rice" begins to crisp slightly at the edges.
5. Off the heat, stir in the green scallion tops and the toasted
   coconut flakes. Taste and adjust salt.

### Modern variation

No breadfruit? Use cauliflower rice as a straight stand-in. The
flavor profile is different (cauliflower is more cabbage-y) but
the technique transfers exactly. Add 1 tsp lime zest at the end
to compensate.

### What to plate it with

A natural base for **2.1 ahi poke** or **5.4 poke bowl, four
ways**. Also works as a side to **5.3 coconut curry with taro**.
